# Announcing the 2020 Go Developer Survey

Every year we ask the Go community a simple question: *How is it going?* Today we're opening the 2020 Go Developer Survey. If you write Go, whether you've shipped production services for a decade or wrote your first `func main()` last week, we want to hear from you.

**[Take the 2020 Go Developer Survey →](#)**

The survey takes about 10 to 15 minutes and will be open for the next few weeks. Every response matters, so please share the link with your colleagues, your meetup group, and anyone else who works with Go.

## Why we run this survey

Go is shaped by the people who use it. Issue trackers, mailing lists, and conference hallways tell us a lot, but they mostly reflect the most vocal parts of the community. The annual survey gives us a broader and more representative view. It shows how developers actually use Go day to day, where they struggle, and what they'd like to see next.

Past surveys have directly influenced our priorities. Feedback about dependency management helped push Go modules from an experiment to the default. Developers repeatedly told us that editor tooling was a pain point, and that drove much of our investment in `gopls`, the Go language server. Error handling and generics showed up again and again as top requests, and those comments informed the design discussions now underway. When you tell us something is hard, we listen, and the survey is one of the clearest ways to make sure we hear it.

## What's different this year

2020 has been an unusual year for nearly everyone. Many teams moved to remote work overnight, conferences went virtual, and the ways developers learn, collaborate, and get help changed quickly. We've updated the survey to reflect that reality.

This year we're especially interested in:

- **Your development environment.** Which editors, IDEs, and tools do you rely on? How well do they work with modules? What's missing?
- **How you learn and find help.** With fewer in-person events, where do you go when you're stuck? Which documentation, tutorials, and community spaces are actually useful?
- **Modules in practice.** Modules have been the recommended approach for a while now. We want to understand the friction that remains, especially around private repositories, proxies, and large codebases.
- **What you're building.** Cloud services, CLIs, data pipelines, embedded systems: Go turns up in more places every year, and knowing where helps us decide what to focus on.
- **The challenges that matter most to you.** We'll ask you to rank the biggest obstacles you face so we can tell what's mildly annoying from what's actually blocking.

We've also trimmed and reorganized several sections based on feedback that last year's survey ran a little long. Many questions are optional, so you can skip anything that doesn't apply to you.

## Who should take it

Everyone who uses Go. We mean that literally. We want responses from:

- Developers who use Go at work, in side projects, or both
- People who are evaluating Go or have only just started learning it
- Developers who tried Go and decided it wasn't the right fit, because knowing *why* is just as valuable
- Engineers working in large organizations, small startups, open source projects, and everything in between

You don't need to be an expert, and there are no wrong answers. Some of the most useful insights come from people who are new to the language and still notice the rough edges that experienced users have learned to work around.

## Privacy and how we use your data

The survey is anonymous by default. We don't ask for your name, and we don't link responses to your identity. Some questions offer free-text fields. Please don't include personal information in them.

Once the survey closes, we'll analyze the results and publish a summary on this blog, as we do every year. We'll share the key trends, highlight what changed since last year, and explain how the findings will shape our roadmap. Aggregated results help the whole ecosystem. Tool authors, library maintainers, educators, and companies that build on Go all use them to understand the community they serve.

## Thank you

Last year more than ten thousand of you took time out of your day to tell us about your experience with Go. That response was humbling, and it made the language and its tooling better for everyone. We're grateful for every answer, every thoughtful comment, and every bug report that grew out of something someone first mentioned in a survey.

So grab a coffee, block out fifteen minutes, and tell us how it's going. And if you can, pass the link along. The more voices we hear, the better we can build the Go that you need.

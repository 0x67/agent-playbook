# Participate in the 2017 Go User Survey

Go has become one of the core languages in our stack. Our API gateway, most of our internal tooling, and a growing share of our data pipeline are written in it. When we adopted Go, we were betting on more than a language. We were also betting on the community and the team that maintain it. Once a year, that team asks the people who write Go every day a simple question: how is it going?

The 2017 Go User Survey is now open. We're encouraging every engineer on our team, and every Go developer reading this, to take it.

## Why a Language Needs a Survey

It's hard to get good feedback on a programming language. Bug reports tell maintainers what's broken. Proposals tell them what a small, motivated group wants. Neither says much about the silent majority: people quietly shipping services, fighting dependency issues on a Friday afternoon, or trying to convince a skeptical team to try Go.

The survey reaches those people at scale. It asks who you are, what you build, where Go fits in your stack, and what makes your work harder than it should be. Across thousands of responses, the answers add up to a picture the Go team can't get from issue trackers or conference conversations.

## What Last Year Taught Us

The first survey ran in 2016. Thousands of developers responded, and the results were published openly on the Go blog. Several themes stood out, and anyone who writes Go professionally will recognize them:

- **Dependency management** was one of the most common pain points. Many of us had our own workarounds, vendoring scripts, and tribal knowledge about which tool to use.
- **Generics** came up again and again, both as a requested feature and as a topic people felt strongly about in each direction.
- **Learning resources and documentation** were valued highly. Respondents also pointed out gaps for intermediate developers moving beyond the basics.
- **Overall satisfaction** was high. Most respondents said they enjoyed working in Go and would recommend it.

These results are more than a report card. The Go team has said publicly that survey responses help decide where they spend their time. You may agree or disagree with any particular decision. Either way, it's better for those decisions to rest on input from a broad range of users than on whoever is loudest online.

## Why We're Asking Our Team to Participate

We have a selfish reason, and we'll say it plainly. We run Go in production across many services, maintained by engineers with very different levels of experience. If companies like ours don't respond, our needs are underrepresented. We can't complain later that the language doesn't fit how we work.

We also have a less selfish reason. Some of our most useful perspectives come from people who aren't Go experts. Several engineers on our team learned Go during onboarding in the past year. Their sense of what was confusing, what clicked, and what still feels awkward is exactly the feedback the language maintainers need. Long-time users have often stopped noticing those rough edges.

So here is our internal policy: take the survey during work hours. It counts as work. Fifteen or twenty minutes of honest feedback is a good investment in a tool we depend on daily.

## What to Expect

The survey covers a range of topics, including:

- Your background and how long you've used Go
- What you build with it and where it runs
- The editors, tools, and libraries you depend on
- How you learn and where you go for help
- What you'd most like to see improved

Responses are anonymous. Many questions are multiple choice, and there are free-text fields where you can elaborate.

## Tips for Giving Useful Feedback

From our experience reading survey results, both ours and others', a few habits make responses much more valuable:

1. **Be specific in free-text answers.** "Tooling could be better" is hard to act on. "I can't easily find which module introduced a transitive dependency" is a concrete problem someone can solve.
2. **Describe how you actually use Go.** Answer based on your real day-to-day work, not on how you'd like to be using it.
3. **Don't skip it because you're new or only use Go occasionally.** Occasional users and newcomers are hard to reach through other channels, so their input is especially valuable.
4. **Mention your context.** Building a CLI tool for fun and running a fleet of microservices lead to very different needs, and that context helps the survey team interpret your answers.

## How to Take It

The survey is linked from the official Go blog at blog.golang.org. It's only open for a limited time, so check the announcement for the closing date and don't put it off. If you work with other Go developers, inside our company or outside it, please pass it along. The more representative the responses, the more useful the results will be for everyone.

Go got to where it is because its users kept telling the people who build it what was working and what wasn't. This is a few minutes of your time to keep doing that.

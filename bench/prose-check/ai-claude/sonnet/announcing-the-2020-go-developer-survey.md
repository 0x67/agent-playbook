# Announcing the 2020 Go Developer Survey

Every year, the Go team asks the community the same basic question: how is Go working for you, and where could it work better? Today we're opening the 2020 Go Developer Survey, and we'd like to hear from you, whether you write Go every day, touch it once a quarter, or are still deciding whether to try it.

**[Take the survey →](#)**

It takes about 15 minutes to complete, and it's open for the next three weeks.

## Why we run this survey

Engineering decisions made without data tend to reflect the loudest voices in the room. Issue trackers, mailing lists, and conference hallway conversations are all valuable, but they skew toward people who are already deeply involved in the project. The survey gives us a way to hear from everyone else: developers at small startups, engineers maintaining internal tools at large enterprises, students writing their first program, and people who tried Go and went back to something else.

Past surveys have directly shaped our priorities. Feedback on dependency management helped drive the work on modules. Repeated requests for better error handling and generic programming kept those topics on our design agenda, even when progress was slow. Responses about editor and tooling experiences have informed how we invest in language servers and debugging support.

We take the results seriously, and we publish them so the whole community can see what we see.

## What we're asking about

This year's survey covers a broad range of topics. A few areas we're especially interested in:

**Your development environment.** Which editors, operating systems, and deployment targets do you use? Are you building for the cloud, the command line, embedded devices, or something else entirely? Understanding where Go code actually runs helps us decide where to focus testing and optimization.

**Modules and dependencies.** Modules have been the default for a while now. We want to know what's working, what's still confusing, and where your workflow still has rough edges, particularly around private repositories, vendoring, and version selection.

**Language features.** We'll ask about the pain points you hit most often, including error handling and the absence of generics. Your answers help us gauge how much these issues affect real projects, not just how often they come up in discussion threads.

**Tooling and diagnostics.** How do you profile, debug, and trace your programs? Which tools do you reach for, and which ones do you avoid because they're hard to set up?

**Community and onboarding.** What was your experience learning Go? Did you find the documentation, tutorials, and community spaces welcoming and useful? If you're new, what almost stopped you?

We've also kept a handful of open-ended questions. They take longer to answer, but the free-text responses are often where we learn the most. A single well-described frustration can reveal a problem our multiple-choice options never anticipated.

## How we handle your responses

We're careful with what you share. Responses are used for aggregate analysis, and we don't publish individual answers. Any free-text comments quoted in our write-up are reviewed to make sure they don't identify the person who wrote them. You can skip any question you'd rather not answer, and you don't need to provide contact details to participate.

When the survey closes, we'll publish a summary of the findings along with the anonymized dataset where possible, so researchers and community members can run their own analyses. We'd rather you check our conclusions than simply trust them.

## Help us reach more Go developers

A survey is only as representative as the people who respond. If the only respondents are those who follow the Go blog, we'll end up with a picture of the most engaged slice of the community and miss everyone else.

You can help in a few ways:

- **Share the link** with teammates, particularly those who use Go but don't follow the project closely.
- **Post it in your community** forums, chat channels, and local meetup groups.
- **Encourage newcomers** to respond. Their perspective on what's confusing is something experienced users can no longer easily see.
- **Reach out to developers who've moved on from Go.** Understanding why people leave is just as informative as understanding why they stay.

## What happens next

The survey closes in three weeks. After that, we'll analyze the responses, and we expect to publish the results in the weeks that follow. We'll share what we learned, what surprised us, and what we plan to do about it.

Thank you for taking the time to participate. Every response, even a short one, makes the picture a little clearer, and helps us build a language that works better for the people who use it.
